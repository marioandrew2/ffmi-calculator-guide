FFMI vs BMI: Which Number Should Lifters Actually Trust?

Two lifters walk into a gym. Both are 6'0" and 200 lbs. One has visible abs and deadlifts twice his bodyweight; the other hasn't trained in years. BMI gives them the exact same score — 27.2, stamped "overweight." If that sounds broken, it's because it is. For anyone who lifts, there's a better number: FFMI.

What BMI Actually Measures (and Why Lifters Break It)

BMI is simple: your weight in kilograms divided by your height in meters, squared. It was designed in the 1830s as a population-level screening tool, and for average sedentary people it works fine. Its one blind spot is also its biggest: it cannot tell muscle from fat. A pound of muscle and a pound of fat score identically, so the more muscle you build, the more "overweight" BMI says you are.

What FFMI Measures Instead

FFMI stands for Fat Free Mass Index. Instead of scoring your total weight, it scores only your lean mass — muscle, bone, water, organs — against your height. The math strips out your fat first:

FFMI = (weight × (1 − body fat %)) ÷ height²

A normalized version then adjusts everyone to a 1.8 m (5'11") reference height, so a 6'4" lifter and a 5'7" lifter can be compared fairly. The result answers the question lifters actually care about — not "am I heavy?" but "how muscular am I for my size?"

FFMI vs BMI, Side by Side

BMI measures total weight against height. Best for: general population screening. Fails for: anyone with meaningful muscle mass.

FFMI measures lean mass against height. Best for: lifters, athletes, coaches, anyone tracking muscle gain. Fails for: nothing in the gym — though it needs an honest body fat percentage input, which is where most people go wrong.

The FFMI Scale, Briefly

For men: under 18 is below average, 18–20 average, 20–22 above average (where consistent lifters land), 22–23 excellent, 23–26 superior. Women run about three points lower across the board. Research on elite bodybuilders (Kouri et al., 1995) found values above roughly 25–26 in men to be rare without performance-enhancing drugs — useful context, not a verdict. For most natural lifters, the scale is a personal benchmark, not a competition.

So Which One Should You Use?

If you don't train, BMI is fine. If you lift, FFMI is the number that reflects your effort. And the way to use it is as a trend, not a single reading: calculate it once, train for 8–12 weeks, then calculate it again under the same conditions — same body fat method, morning, fasted, before training. Climbing means the program works. Flat for six months means something needs fixing.

One hard rule on inputs: never guess your body fat percentage. A 5% miss moves your FFMI by nearly a full point. The US Navy tape method is free, takes two minutes, and beats a guess every time.
